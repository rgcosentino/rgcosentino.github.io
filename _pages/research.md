My research asks how energy and momentum are transported and redistributed inside planetary atmospheres. A unifying thread is using things we **can** observe, such as temperature fields, cloud motions and Doppler-shifted spectral lines, to constrain things we **can't**, such as vertical wind shear and the deep forcing that drives jets and storms. Together, the outer planets form a natural laboratory for fluid dynamics under extreme conditions, and a comparative framework that extends naturally to exoplanets.

The deep atmospheres of the giant planets are where convection, wave generation and jet forcing begin, yet optically thick clouds hide them from view. Building on Earth atmospheric science, especially wave–mean flow interaction and Earth's Quasi-Biennial Oscillation, I showed that upper-atmosphere temperatures retrieved from infrared spectroscopy can constrain otherwise unobservable vertical wind shear through the thermal wind equation.

Applying this to Jupiter, my general circulation modeling showed that waves generated by convection in the weather layer can drive the reversals of Jupiter's equatorial jet and its downward-propagating shear zones, explaining the **quasi-quadrennial oscillation** ([Cosentino et al. 2017](/publications/)). Related work showed that vertical wind shear below the clouds controls the stability and shape of features such as the **Jovian ribbon** and **Saturn's hexagon**.

Facilities like **ALMA** and the **VLA** offer resolution, wavelength coverage and cadence that are hard to get from spacecraft alone, yet they remain underused for planetary science. With the VLA, I showed that radio wavelengths probing beneath Jupiter's visible clouds reveal features with no optical counterpart and a two-dimensional turbulence signature of a shallow weather layer. With **Hubble**, I extended the turbulence analysis to show that Jupiter's bright zones and dark belts are powered differently: zones by jet-scale (Rhines-scale) dynamics, belts by convective storms.

As PI of successive ALMA programs, I've built **multi-year, high-cadence datasets of stratospheric winds** on Jupiter and Saturn, the highest-resolution wind measurements of Neptune to date, and observations of Uranus' polar region, alongside long-term ALMA monitoring of **Titan's** winds with collaborators.

## Open, reproducible wind measurements

Previous Doppler wind studies of Jupiter, Saturn and Neptune relied on proprietary software, making results hard to reproduce. I'm developing an open-source, planet-agnostic **Doppler wind retrieval toolkit** in [pylanetary](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.7608476) that extracts velocity fields from spectral lines such as CO and HCN, so wind measurements can be made the same way across every planet.

## Looking ahead

- **A climate record for the giant planets.** Sustained ALMA monitoring of Jupiter and Saturn, with intraseasonal monitoring of Neptune, to build the first systematic record of jet variability beyond Earth.
- **Seasons on the ice giants.** Following Uranus' north polar region toward its ~2030 northern summer solstice to track the evolution of its polar cyclone, in support of future Uranus mission planning.
- **Solar System science with Roman.** Turning moving objects in Roman's wide-field surveys from contaminants into science: catalogs of small bodies, planetary defense ([Holler, Cosentino et al. 2025](/publications/)), and giant-planet satellite environments such as the Io plasma torus.
